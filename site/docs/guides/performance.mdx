title: Performance
description: Where the frame budget goes, which paths copy, and what to measure before deciding anything is slow.
---

The engine is built to a budget per frame rather than to a target frame rate,
because a frame that arrives late is worse than a frame that did less.

## The budget

A session carries `frame_budget_us`: the ceiling for everything the engine does
between a frame arriving and the result being ready. Zero means the engine
picks from what the device can sustain.

When the work will not fit, the engine does not drop the frame. It climbs down
the [degradation ladder](/docs/concepts/degradation) in a fixed, documented
order, so the thing that gets worse is the thing you chose in advance rather
than whatever happened to be last in the queue.

Set the budget from the refresh rate you are drawing at, leaving the
application its own share. A budget that consumes the whole frame interval
leaves nothing for the compositor and shows up as judder that profiles blame
on the engine.

## What copies and what does not

The common path copies nothing. A frame arrives as a platform texture or a
hardware buffer, is imported, and is used where it is.

The copying paths exist and are correct, and they are there for bringing a
platform up rather than for shipping:

- A planes-and-strides submit stages the frame through the engine's own
  staging pool.
- An RGBA copy submit converts on the way in.

If a profile shows time in staging on a platform that has a shared texture
path, the integration is on the wrong call rather than the engine being slow.

## Allocation

Nothing in the engine allocates per frame on the common path. Pools are sized
at creation and reused.

A binding can undo that on its own, and usually does so in one of two ways:
building a fresh descriptor object per frame, or materialising the record into
new objects per frame. Reuse the descriptor. Hand the record back as a view
where your language allows one, and as a reused buffer where it does not.

## Pool exhaustion is a signal

`GOSS_ERROR_POOL_EXHAUSTED` means every buffer is still held by the caller. It
is a backpressure signal rather than a failure: stop pulling frames until the
engine has caught up.

Raising the capacity is occasionally right and usually postpones the problem.
A pool twice the size exhausts twice as late and stalls for twice as long.

## Measuring

Read the engine's own numbers rather than wrapping calls in timers. A wall
clock around a submit says the frame took eight milliseconds; the node report
says which node spent six of them.

```c
goss_degrade_level level = goss_session_report_frame(session, frame_time_us, thermal);
```

That one call both drives the ladder and tells you where it settled. Feed it
your platform's real thermal state; see [degradation](/docs/concepts/degradation).

Then take the engine section of the record, which carries the budget, what was
spent, the degrade level, `degrade_transitions`, `nodes_degraded` and
`node_reports_lost`:

```c
goss_session_perception_json(session, GOSS_PERCEPTION_ENGINE, buf, cap, &written);
```

Three numbers tell you most of what you need:

- **spent against limit.** Headroom, or the lack of it.
- **degrade_transitions.** A number that keeps climbing means flapping, and
  flapping costs more than sitting one step lower.
- **node_reports_lost.** The measurements themselves are incomplete.

Measure cold, because the first frame is the one the user waits on. Measure
again after several minutes, because a device that is comfortable for thirty
seconds and throttled at three minutes has a different problem.

## Before shipping

- The budget is set from the refresh rate, not left at a guess.
- The platform's zero-copy submit is the one in use, confirmed by a profile
  rather than by reading the code.
- Nothing allocates per frame in the binding.
- Exhaustion produces backpressure, proven by holding frames on purpose.
- The device is still cool after several minutes of continuous use.
